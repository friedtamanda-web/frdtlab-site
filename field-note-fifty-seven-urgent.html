FRDTLAB · Field Note · Agentic operations

Fifty-seven things are marked urgent.

By Amanda Friedt · November 27, 2026

A hundred and thirty things are open on my plate right now, not this month. Here is the agentic AI system I built to run four kids, three schools, a business, and a house, and the child it found that I had not counted yet.

AI workflow governance

Where the humans stand

  • The question every room asks: how do people stay involved. The answer is gates, not vibes.
  • Agent Output: draft, feed, campaign, report
  • Gate 1 · Confidence Scoring: the agent rates its own certainty
  • Gate 2 · Source Validation: where did this claim come from
  • Gate 3 · Research Verification: facts checked against primary data
  • Gate 4 · QA Loop: a second agent hunts for errors
  • Gate 5 · Human Review: judgment, taste, brand truth
  • Gate 6 · Approval Checkpoint: a person signs before it ships
  • Ships: with a paper trail
  • Exception handling: anything below the confidence threshold skips the line and routes straight to a human queue. Autonomy is earned, never assumed.
  • The gates are the trust. Speed comes from the agents; credibility comes from the checkpoints.

Written from a little further out, with the benefit of knowing which parts mattered.

I did not arrive at this through a clean productivity experiment. I gave up on laundry and downloaded an app. Then I opened my inbox and laughed, because the inbox was running the same play: about forty separate systems, all of them quietly assuming my brain was the integration layer. So I went and counted what I was actually holding right now, today, not cumulatively. A hundred and thirty live actions. Fifty-seven of them marked urgent, which is not a priority system, it's a queue with good lighting. And buried in the pile, a fourth child whose school provides every supply, which nobody had told me, because I had never gotten far enough down the list to ask.

That last part is the whole story. I'll come back to it.

The haunted switchboard of invisible labor

School message. Teacher note. ParentSquare ding. Target receipt. Walmart shipping update. Soccer schedule. Co-parenting question. Tax email. Insurance follow-up. Calendar invite. Bill notice. Package update.

Every one of those looks manageable alone. That's the trick. Individually they're all reasonable. Together they become a switchboard nobody is staffing except you.

I counted the sources. Conservatively, my life pulls from about forty of them. Thirteen for kids and school, from the district calendar to the bus assignments to the placement PDFs. Seven for shopping, because Drive Up and shipping and receipts are three different systems pretending to be one. Five for co-parenting. Five for work. Ten for the house and the money.

And that's before the soft signals, which are the expensive ones. Did someone confirm that, or did I assume it. Was that a real date or a placeholder. Did I already buy this. Which kid was that for. Did it ship, or is it sitting at Drive Up. Why is a purple folder suddenly the emotional center of my life.

Invisible labor isn't one big task. It's forty small systems, each one quietly appointing you the middleware.

I counted, and it was worse than I guessed

I told someone there were probably a hundred things. Then I opened the workbook and pulled the actual rows.

A hundred and thirty live actions. A hundred and twenty-five of them still open.

I want to be precise about what that number is, because the sloppy version of this story is more flattering to me and I don't want it. That is not a month of output. My completed work lives in a separate archive and it is long. This is the current docket. It's what is open on my plate as of this morning, running concurrently, all of it waiting on some decision or action from me.

The number is not a measure of how little I finish. It's a measure of how much I hold at once.

And then there are the twenty sitting in Waiting. Those are the ones where I already did the work. I sent the email, I made the call, I joined the list. The task is still mine, because now my job is remembering to ask again. Taxes aren't done because I sent the email. The insurance claim isn't done because I made the call. Childcare isn't solved because I joined a waitlist.

Waiting isn't resting. Waiting is unpaid follow-up labor with a nicer name.

What the outsourcing actually did

I've been buying my way out of pieces of this. Laundry app. Cleaners. Lawn care. A home service came this week.

The service had a price. The sanity was the actual purchase.

But here's what nobody warns you about, and I'd want any leader reading this to sit with it for a second. Outsourcing doesn't remove the executive function. It converts it. The task stops being do the labor and becomes manage the vendor, the money, the timing, the quality, the access, and the follow-up.

I didn't build an agentic system because I wanted to be more productive. I built it because even the outsourced labor still needed a project manager, and apparently that was also me.

Outsourcing didn't make me less capable. It made me honest about the fact that my capability had become the default operating budget for the entire house.

There was a kid in the inbox

Here's the part I almost didn't publish.

I spent roughly two hours building a very good back-to-school system. Verified product links, per-color folder mapping, price reconciliation across two retailers, a live checklist my kids' father could work from. Genuinely good work.

Then I searched my own email, and found a fourth child.

Not lost. Not neglected. Just not in scope, because I had built the scope from a supply list instead of from my life. My four year old is in preschool. His program provides every single supply, breakfast, lunch, and snack, which meant the correct amount of shopping for him was zero, and I would have found that out on the first day.

The email had been sitting there for four days.

That's not a story about being a bad mother. It's a story about what happens when the load exceeds what a human integration layer can hold. The signal was in the system. There was just no system reading it.

What I actually built: AI for the mental load

Not a chatbot that answers questions. An operating layer. The plumbing underneath it all.

Signals come in. School emails, ParentSquare, teacher notes, placement PDFs, supply lists, Target and Walmart orders, calendar invites, AYSO, childcare, texts, old spreadsheets. Instead of rereading all of it, the layer pulls only what creates an obligation, a deadline, a dependency, or a decision.

Then it reconciles, which is the part that matters. Extraction is easy now; anyone can do extraction. Reconciliation is the work. My purchasing had to distinguish between items ordered, items still missing, items sitting at Drive Up, items shipping to the house, and items arriving the same day as Open House. That last one isn't an order status. It's a timing risk, and it only appears if something is comparing two sources against each other.

It also has to catch its own errors. Three of my conclusions were wrong until the receipt corrected them. That isn't a flaw in the design; that is the design. A system that can't be corrected by a newer source is just a confident guess with a spreadsheet.

Then it routes. Task, calendar, ledger, follow-up loop, draft message, or reference only. Six destinations, and choosing correctly is most of the value.

The calendar only gets confirmed dates. AYSO is Tuesday practices and Saturday games, but the team times aren't out yet, so those sit as clearly labeled placeholders rather than pretending to be real. A system that puts guesses on your calendar is worse than no system, because now you can't trust any of it.

Money and tasks stay separate. A task says follow up on the insurance claim. A ledger answers what the repair cost, what insurance paid, what the deductible was, who paid, and whether anyone is reimbursing part of it. They connect; they aren't the same object. Collapsing them is how people lose real money inside a text thread.

Nothing sends without me. The layer reads, extracts, reconciles, prioritizes, drafts, tracks, calculates, and surfaces conflicts. I still decide what gets sent, whether a credit is acceptable, whether a contractor is hired, whether an inferred date is trustworthy enough to be real.

That's the line I'd defend hardest. Winning with this stuff is just as much about trust and judgment as it is the tech. You have to understand the humans and the system; it's when they're integrated that you see anything work. A layer that removes coordination burden is infrastructure. A layer that removes judgment is a liability with good branding.

Childcare is a revenue constraint

This reframe changed more than any tool did.

My four year old's preschool day runs 8:00 to 3:00, and aftercare isn't available; he's on a waitlist. If I personally handle both ends of that, my actual workday shrinks to something under six hours once you count the driving.

That isn't a family calendar problem. That's a revenue constraint.

Once you model it that way, you work backward from the hours you need available instead of discovering the conflict at 7:40 on a Tuesday. The system tracks confirmed coverage, requested coverage, paid coverage, waitlist status, drop-offs, pickups, known gaps, and backups.

Every parent doing this math in their head is running capacity planning without calling it that, and without getting credit for it. I'd guess a fair number of the people reading this are doing exactly that right now, in a meeting, while nodding.

What it caught

Four kids, three schools, two retailers, two open house nights, one week. Total spend, a hundred and forty-four dollars and twelve cents.

The shopping was never the value. Seven things surfaced that would have failed quietly:

Identical Ticonderoga pencils at $5.59 or $40.53, depending on which listing you tapped, because marketplace sellers ride the search results. Play-Doh at $3.48 from the store and $13.99 from a reseller sitting on top of it. Nine folders ordered when ten were needed, visible only by counting the receipt line by line. Pink headphones linked for a six-year-old boy. An event that had already happened. A fourth child, already covered.

And the one that mattered most: two open houses on two different nights. The middle school runs Monday. The elementary school runs Wednesday. I had them collapsed into a single date, which would have meant the right supplies at the wrong building, with the wrong kid, on the wrong evening.

That was caught by reading a calendar. Not by shopping harder.

The purple folder was never the problem. The problem was that the purple folder had dependencies.

The part I got wrong

I searched my inbox last.

Every load-bearing fact lived there. The fourth kid. The real open house dates. The half-day start. The teacher's name. Two hours of product research came before the ten minutes that reframed the entire week.

That's a sequencing error, not a tooling one, and I'm naming it because it's the most common failure I see in real system design, including in rooms where everyone has a title. We optimize the visible task before validating the scope of the actual task. I did it in my own kitchen with my own children, which is about as low-stakes an environment as I'm ever going to get to learn it in.

I'm not organized. I'm instrumented.

Somebody is going to read this and comment that they could never be that on top of things.

I want to be clear about what actually happened here, because I think the distinction matters for anyone running a team, a company, or a house, and increasingly for the people doing all three.

I am not organized. I have a hundred and twenty-five open items and fifty-seven of them are marked urgent. By any traditional read, that's a person underwater.

I'm instrumented. There's a difference, and only one of those survives a bad week.

Organized is a state you maintain with willpower, and willpower is the first thing that goes when a kid gets sick or a client escalates or the roof leaks. Instrumented means the state lives outside your body. It means an email can change a deadline, and the deadline can move a calendar event, and the calendar event can break childcare, and childcare can shrink work availability, and work availability can determine which house projects happen, and the project can create an expense, and the expense can land in a shared ledger, and none of that has to route through my memory at two in the morning.

Those aren't eleven separate problems. That's one cross-system workflow, and I was the only thing connecting it.

I didn't publish the hundred and thirty because I'm comfortable with it. I published it because I've never once seen anyone show their real concurrent load, and I've spent enough years watching capable people quietly assume they're the only one holding this much.

You're not behind. You're the integration layer for about forty systems that were never designed to talk to each other, and nobody staffed the role.

That's the work I care about now, and it's who I want to be building with: founders, operators, and leadership teams who are done pretending the coordination is free. Meaningful momentum, not more noise. Roll your sleeves up and go find where the signal is actually breaking.

The complexity isn't going anywhere. Four kids, three schools, a house, a business, and a co-parenting arrangement will keep generating it, and honestly, so will your org chart.

The agentic layer just gives that complexity somewhere to go besides my head.

A system that keeps running when I'm not in the room.

Sidebar · The case study

Running back-to-school through an agent

TL;DR: Four kids, three schools, two retailers, two open-house nights, one week. Handled as a system instead of a series of errands. Total spend $144.12. The value wasn't the shopping. It was the seven things that would have failed silently.

The real problem

Back-to-school isn't a shopping problem. It's a reconciliation problem wearing a shopping problem's clothes.

The supply list is one input. The store's actual inventory is another. The receipt is a third. The school's calendar is a fourth. Your email is a fifth. Every one of them is authoritative about something and wrong about something else, and none of them talk to each other.

The failure mode isn't forgetting to buy glue sticks. It's buying nine folders when you needed ten and not finding out until a first grader is standing in a classroom.

The five moves that mattered

1. Structure the unstructured input first. A pasted supply list became a reconciled table before anything got bought. Quantities collapsed across kids (9 glue sticks → one 12-pack), pack sizes resolved against real SKUs, conflicts surfaced early.

2. Never trust a generated link. Every product URL was fetched and confirmed before it went in a list. One guessed product ID resolved to a Lilo & Stitch t-shirt. That's the whole argument for verification as a standing rule rather than a spot check.

3. Go to the source, not the summary. The district's own PDF. The teacher's own email. The actual receipt. Not memory, not inference, not "usually schools do X."

4. Reconcile against reality, then correct in public. When the receipt arrived, three of my earlier conclusions were wrong. Ticonderoga pencils were in the order. So were the binder, ruler, calculator and pouch: they sat in a shipping group the screenshots didn't cover. Those corrections got written into the artifact, not buried.

5. Let each artifact feed the next. The verified product links from kid one became the starting inventory for kid four. Twenty items, and only a handful needed fresh research.

What the system caught

Seven things that were invisible from inside the task:

  • Pack-size arbitrage. Identical Ticonderoga pencils: $5.59 for 30, $18.99 for 36, $40.53 for 60. The expensive ones were marketplace sellers riding Target's search results.
  • Reseller pricing. Play-Doh at $13.99 from a third party vs $3.48 sold by Walmart. Nearly every 4-pack in search was marked up 3–5x.
  • A folder short. Ten needed, nine ordered. Only visible by counting the receipt line by line.
  • The wrong colorway. I linked pink headphones for a six-year-old boy without checking.
  • A missing kid. A fourth child surfaced only when the email got searched: in preschool, whose program provides every supply. Zero shopping required.
  • An event already passed. The school's welcome event was the day before, not the coming week.
  • An open house on the wrong night. The middle school runs Monday. The elementary school runs Wednesday. Those had been collapsed into one date, which would have meant showing up with the wrong kid's supplies on the wrong evening.

That last one is the one that matters. It was caught by checking a calendar, not by shopping harder.

The pattern, generalized

Structure the input → verify every external claim → reconcile against the source of record → correct openly → let outputs compound.

Nothing here is specific to school supplies. It's the same loop as a campaign audit, a vendor reconciliation, or a data migration. The domain changed; the system didn't.

The leverage isn't speed. A person can shop faster than this took. The leverage is that the failure modes get surfaced before they cost anything, and that the artifact keeps working after the conversation ends.

What I'd do differently

Search the email first, not last. Every genuinely load-bearing fact (the fourth kid, the real open house dates, the half-day start, the teacher's name, the "all supplies provided") came from email and the district calendar. Two hours of product research preceded the ten minutes that reframed the whole week.

The lesson isn't "check email." It's that I optimized the visible task before validating the scope of the actual task. Classic sequencing error, and worth naming.
