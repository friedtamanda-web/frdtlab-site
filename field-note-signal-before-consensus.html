Signal Before Consensus: The Leaders Who Stay Calm Will See It First

By Amanda Friedt · November 27, 2026

There is a version of leadership that gets rewarded in chaotic times. It looks fast. Urgent. Decisive.

How you run it every week

Signal → decision → action. No 47-slide readout.

  • Weekly signal standup.
  • What changed? search terms, SERPs, AI answers, feed errors
  • What did we learn? which signal moved the next decision
  • What do we ship? one fix, one asset, one paid test
  • 30-day rollout.
  • Week 1 map decisions + query gaps
  • Week 2 fix taxonomy / feed / landing friction
  • Week 3 launch paid tests + AI-answer assets
  • Week 4 build the feedback loop + scorecard

The short version.

  • Calm is not passive. Not slow. The next era will reward the leader who can tell whether something is a signal at all.
  • Receipts over reactions. One headline is a story. Six weeks is a pattern. Evidence, then verdict.
  • Track upstream, not everything. Ten categories: capital, constraints, labor language, customer discovery, the consumer beneath the average, freight, pricing power, organizational redesign, what breaks and language before consensus.
  • Run it as a loop and a weekly rhythm. Notice, place, compare, verify, decide, architect, communicate. A team with a signal rhythm compounds.
  • Calm creates compounding. Panic creates motion. Calm creates accumulation.

Every Slack message gets an immediate response. Every headline becomes a meeting. Every platform update becomes a new strategy. Every new AI tool gets tested. Every competitor announcement creates a small internal crisis.

Everyone is moving. Everyone is busy. Everyone’s hair is on fire.

And from the outside, that can look a lot like leadership.

I have written about this before, long before I started connecting energy grids, data centers, earnings calls, search behavior and capital flows.

In my leadership writing, I wrote:

The best leaders I’ve worked with stay calm when everything else is chaos. Not because they have all the answers, but because panic never solves the problem.

I didn’t think of that as a marketing thesis when I wrote it. I do now.

Because I think one of the defining competitive advantages of the next several years will be the ability to remain intellectually calm while the environment becomes increasingly noisy.

Not passive. Not slow. Calm.

There is a difference.

Calm is not the absence of urgency

My journal said it even more simply:

I don’t react the same anymore. I don’t chase the same things. I see more. I pause more.

And then:

There is a steadiness that I didn’t have before. Not loud. Not forced. Just consistent. I trust that more than intensity.

That language feels unexpectedly relevant to where marketing is headed.

Because we are entering an environment that rewards reactivity almost perfectly.

  • Another AI model launches.
  • Google changes something.
  • A new agent can supposedly replace half a workflow.
  • Traffic falls.
  • A competitor goes viral.
  • A CEO reads an article about AI and wants a strategy by Friday.
  • Another dashboard shows a metric going the wrong direction.
  • Another vendor tells you everything you built is obsolete.
  • Another analyst declares the death of search.
  • Another analyst declares the death of SaaS.
  • Another analyst declares the death of marketing.

And somebody somewhere is already posting the 17 things your company should have implemented yesterday.

The easiest thing for a leader to do in that environment is react. The harder thing is to see.

I see more. I pause more.

That might actually be an operating model.

Because pausing is not the same as waiting.

Pausing creates enough distance to ask:

  • Is this a signal or noise?
  • Is this downstream evidence of something happening upstream?
  • Is this structural or temporary?
  • What would have to be true for this to matter?
  • What else should be moving if my hypothesis is correct?
  • Where is actual money moving?
  • What behavior changed?
  • What did companies do, not just say?

Those questions have slowly become the center of my work.

And looking back, I can see that I was building toward them for years.

The calm leader is the translator in the room

The calm leader is often the translator in the room.

The one connecting the headline to the revenue model, the tool to the workflow, the metric to the customer behavior, the board question to the team’s capacity.

That is the hidden work. Not just deciding. Translating.

  • Between CEO and team.
  • Between data and fear.
  • Between market signal and internal reaction.
  • Between what changed and what actually needs to change.

That kind of translation rarely looks dramatic from the outside. It does not always produce an immediate answer. It does not always satisfy the room’s demand for certainty.

But it creates the space where the right answer can become visible.

And in a market where every system is moving at once, that translation layer becomes leadership.

Receipts over reactions

One of the quiet rules I keep coming back to is:

Receipts > Reactions.

One headline is a story. Six weeks is a pattern.

One platform screenshot is a signal candidate. A movement across customer behavior, capital allocation, margins, hiring, search demand and product usage is a signal.

Evidence, then verdict. That is the discipline.

It does not mean you ignore early information. It means you refuse to confuse early information with proof.

A screenshot can go in the watch column. A vendor announcement can go in the watch column. A sudden metric shift can go in the watch column.

But a roadmap should require more than a mood. A strategy should require more than a headline. A reorg should require more than fear.

The next era will not reward the leader who reacts fastest to the signal.

It will reward the leader who can tell whether something is a signal at all.

That requires a different kind of operating system:

receipts over reactions,
timelines over hot takes,
patterns over panic,
and enough steadiness to let the evidence become visible before the room demands certainty.

The operating problem

You cannot outwork a broken strategy

Another line from my earlier leadership work:

You can’t outwork a broken strategy. If the system is wrong, no amount of effort will fix it.

That feels almost painfully applicable to marketing in 2026.

Teams are being asked to produce more.

More content. More personalization. More channels. More reporting. More experimentation. More AI adoption. More revenue. More pipeline.

Faster.

Meanwhile, here is what Gartner’s 2026 CMO research says:

Gartner 2026 CMO researchFigure
Marketing budgets as a share of company revenuearound 7.8%
CMOs who say they lack the budget to execute their strategy56%
CMOs who say they lack sufficient resources54%
Average share of budgets CMOs are allocating to AI15.3%
CMOs who describe their organizations as mature enough to scale it30%

Source: Gartner 2026 CMO Spend Survey.

That is an extraordinary management problem.

The answer cannot simply be: work harder.

And it cannot be: add AI.

If the underlying operating system is wrong, AI may simply allow a broken organization to produce broken work faster.

That is already showing up in the language marketing operators use:

  • “The stack is a mess.”
  • “We’re drowning in disconnected tools.”
  • “AI is only as good as the garbage underneath it.”
  • “I spend more time fixing than building.”

That is not primarily a technology problem. It is an architecture problem.

AI did not kill strategy. It exposed who never had an operating system.

You cannot organize chaos while you are arguing with it

You cannot organize chaos while you are arguing with it.

But you can document it.

That is usually where steadiness starts.

  • What happened?
  • When?
  • Who said it mattered?
  • What else moved?
  • What did not move?
  • What would prove this was real?
  • What would prove this was noise?

Documentation turns panic into material. And material can be analyzed.

This is one of the most overlooked forms of executive function in chaotic markets.

The leader does not have to make the chaos disappear. The leader has to make it legible enough that the organization can stop reacting blindly to it.

That is why documentation matters. Not for bureaucracy. For clarity.

A timeline is not paperwork. A timeline is how you keep the organization from rewriting reality every time the mood changes.

The timeline never lies

The timeline never lies.

Not because every moment is obvious when it happens, but because patterns become much harder to manipulate once they are placed in sequence.

  • What was announced?
  • What was funded?
  • What was delayed?
  • What was cut?
  • What got reorganized?
  • What kept showing up in earnings calls?
  • What moved from pilot to budget?
  • What quietly disappeared?

That is why signal work is timeline work.

It is not just noticing events. It is watching sequence.

The announcement matters. The funding matters more. The delayed rollout matters. The hiring pattern matters. The language shift matters. The margin pressure matters. The customer behavior matters. The follow-through matters most.

A leader who only reacts to isolated events will always be at the mercy of the latest headline.

A leader who tracks sequence begins to see what is forming before it has a name.

Calm as a competitive edge

The leader with their hair on fire versus the leader with a flashlight

One of the frameworks in my leadership research is called:

Flashlight, Not Fortune Teller.

I love that more now than when we named it.

Nobody running a marketing organization in 2026 can know exactly what happens next.

Nobody knows precisely how search shakes out. Nobody knows exactly how quickly agents change commerce. Nobody knows the final labor impact of AI. Nobody knows whether today’s scale of AI infrastructure investment produces extraordinary returns, normal returns or an ugly period of overbuilding.

Alibaba is currently illustrating the tension perfectly. Its latest quarter showed strong cloud and AI growth while its massive AI infrastructure investment helped drive a sharp drop in quarterly profit. The company is simultaneously making the argument that the investment is necessary for the future and demonstrating how expensive that future is to build. (Reuters)

There is no clean answer yet.

That’s the point.

Leadership is not knowing the answer before everyone else.

Leadership is being able to illuminate enough of the terrain that people can move without pretending the entire map exists.

Flashlight. Not fortune teller.

And this is where calm becomes competitive

The reactive leader sees an AI headline and launches an AI initiative. The calm leader asks where the economic value actually appears.

The reactive leader sees traffic decline and demands more SEO content. The calm leader asks whether discovery behavior changed.

The reactive leader hears “agents” and buys another tool. The calm leader looks at process maturity, data quality, permissions, governance and where human judgment still matters.

The reactive leader sees a bad quarter and cuts whatever can be cut fastest. The calm leader asks whether the weakness is cyclical, structural or measurement-related.

The reactive leader chases activity. The calm leader follows the system.

That is not softness.

I wrote this years ago:

That is not a soft skill. That is executive function. That is what makes the difference between a leader who builds something lasting and one who burns it down with their own reactivity.

I think that line belongs in the marketing leadership conversation now.

Because marketing leaders are being asked to operationalize ambiguity.

Operationalizing ambiguity

The language in my more recent leadership research is almost painfully accurate:

  • “CFO wants savings, team wants certainty.”
  • “I’m translating chaos all day.”
  • “Leadership says move faster without changing the system.”
  • “I need a board answer for an unsolved question.”
  • “I’m expected to operationalize ambiguity.”

That may be the job description for the next generation of marketing leadership.

Operationalize ambiguity. Not eliminate it. Not perform certainty.

Build enough structure around uncertainty that an organization can act intelligently anyway.

Gartner’s work heading into 2026 was already showing the contradiction. Revenue growth remained the top CMO priority while budget and resource constraints were the largest challenge. Half of surveyed CMOs said short-term needs were interfering with long-term strategic planning. At the same time, AI agents and changing search behavior were forcing leaders to reconsider the marketing operating model itself. (Gartner)

That means the leader who wins cannot simply be the best marketer in the old sense of the word.

They have to become something else.

The marketing leader becomes an interpreter of systems

I have written:

I turn chaos into systems.

For a long time, I thought of that as a description of something I happen to be good at.

Now I think it describes the emerging role.

Marketing leaders increasingly need to understand enough about several interconnected systems to see changes before they arrive neatly labeled as a “marketing trend.”

That includes:

  • capital
  • consumer behavior
  • search
  • AI
  • technology
  • energy
  • infrastructure
  • labor
  • media
  • culture
  • operations
  • sales
  • product
  • data
  • regulation

None of these lives neatly inside the marketing department anymore.

And that brings me back to something I was already writing in 2025:

Infrastructure cues are upstream indicators.

The trick is knowing which ones to watch.

The CMO role is becoming more infrastructural

The CMO role is not becoming less creative.

It is becoming more infrastructural.

The future CMO cannot only understand campaigns.

They have to understand discovery systems, data systems, customer systems, AI systems, sales systems and economic signals.

The job is moving from campaign leadership to market interpretation and growth architecture.

That does not make creativity less important. It makes creative work more dependent on the system around it.

A brilliant campaign cannot compensate forever for bad data, broken follow-up, unclear customer definitions, disconnected product information or a measurement layer that rewards the wrong behavior.

The future marketing leader has to be able to ask:

  • Where does demand originate?
  • Where does discovery happen?
  • What does the machine understand about us?
  • What does the customer believe?
  • Where does the handoff fail?
  • Which signals are being ignored?
  • Which metrics are lying by omission?
  • Which workflows are producing motion without learning?

That is not traditional campaign management.

That is growth systems leadership.

Ten upstream signals

So what should we be tracking now? Ten market signals for CMOs

Not everything.

That would recreate the exact problem we are trying to solve.

I would build the marketing leader’s signal system around a handful of upstream categories.

SignalWhat to watchWhy it matters
1 · Follow capitalWhere companies are committing real money. Not pilots. Not press releases. CapEx, debt, acquisitions, long-term contracts, facilities, hiring, infrastructure.Money creates receipts.
2 · Follow constraintsWhat stops growth: power, grid access, chips, memory, transformers, construction, land, water, permitting, talent, data, integration, governance.A constraint tells us where value may migrate next.
3 · Follow labor languageWhen executives say: productivity, efficiency, operating leverage, flattening, reallocation, disciplined hiring, automation, streamlining.The structure of marketing teams will follow the economics.
4 · Follow customer discoveryAI citations, brand mentions inside answers, direct traffic, branded search, social search, marketplace search, referral patterns from AI interfaces, share of recommendation, zero-click exposure, machine-readable product information, structured entity coverage.Not because rankings stop mattering. Because rankings become one piece of a larger discovery system.
5 · Follow the consumer beneath the averageDifferent consumers, tracked separately: lower income, middle income, affluent, young consumers, families, small businesses, enterprise buyers. Watch trade-down, basket size, credit stress, BNPL, promotions, private label, restaurant frequency, travel, home improvement, subscription churn, delayed purchases.“Consumer spending” is becoming less useful as a single statement. The contradiction between categories may matter more than the headline average.
6 · Follow freightTrucking, rail, ports and shipping are boring until they are not. Freight can help reveal inventory changes, manufacturing demand, consumer demand, reshoring, construction activity, tariff pull-forwards and regional economic changes.They tell you whether physical things are actually moving, before those trends show up cleanly in marketing dashboards.
7 · Follow pricing powerNot simply inflation. Who can still raise prices without losing demand? Who cannot? Who is absorbing tariffs? Who is passing them through? Who suddenly needs promotions?Pricing power tells us a lot about brand strength, consumer health and competitive structure. That is marketing information.
8 · Follow organizational redesignDo not ask only: Which AI tools are companies adopting? Ask: How is work being reorganized because those tools exist?The advantage is an organization structured well enough to use it.
9 · Follow what breaksOutages, failed implementations, bad data, fraud, security incidents, model errors, customer backlash, operational cleanup.Reliability becomes strategy.
10 · Follow language before consensusWatch for new clusters. When ten unrelated CEOs suddenly start saying the same strange thing, write it down.Then go find the behavior underneath it.

Notes on the signals

1. Follow capital

One of the biggest signals right now is the enormous amount of capital flowing into AI infrastructure. Alibaba alone has outlined roughly $56 billion of AI investment through 2029 and is raising additional capital for chips, infrastructure and models. (Reuters)

If executives say something is transformative but refuse to materially fund it, that tells us something.

If they keep funding it despite margin pressure, that tells us something else.

2. Follow constraints

I am increasingly interested in what stops growth.

AI began as a model story. Then it became a chip story. Then a data-center story. Then an electricity story.

It may increasingly become a grid, industrial-equipment, construction and political story.

That is why seemingly unrelated earnings calls matter.

3. Follow labor language

These phrases do not automatically mean layoffs.

But patterns matter.

Marketing leaders should be tracking:

  • revenue per employee
  • headcount growth versus revenue growth
  • which roles companies continue hiring
  • where junior work disappears
  • where senior judgment becomes more valuable
  • where AI produces actual labor substitution versus simply more output

4. Follow customer discovery

The marketing question is no longer simply:

Where is our traffic coming from?

The people in the research are already asking:

  • “What happens if AI owns discovery?”
  • “Will our brand disappear inside answers?”
  • “Traffic is no longer the whole story.”
  • “I don’t know where attention goes next.”

Exactly.

8. Follow organizational redesign

This might be the most important internal signal.

Gartner reports that while 70% of CMOs consider becoming an AI leader critical, 70% also acknowledge that their processes are not mature enough to scale AI effectively. (Gartner)

That gap is the story.

The competitive advantage isn’t access to AI.

Access is rapidly commoditizing.

9. Follow what breaks

One line from the operator research deserves more attention:

Silent errors scare me more than obvious ones.

That will become a major management issue.

As automation increases, the cost of unnoticed mistakes can scale alongside the efficiency gains.

The next leadership risk is not only that AI makes mistakes.

It is that AI makes mistakes quietly, at scale, inside systems that appear to be working.

A broken dashboard is obvious. A hallucinated citation in one report is fixable.

A bad conversion definition feeding automated bidding for six months is different. A lead-quality problem disguised as efficient acquisition is different. A content system producing confident but slightly wrong product guidance is different.

Silent errors scare me more than obvious ones because obvious errors create interruption.

Silent errors create strategy.

10. Follow language before consensus

This is where earnings calls become interesting.

Not because every executive phrase means something profound.

But language changes before PowerPoint frameworks do.

The loop and the rhythm

How to read market signals: the calm leader’s signal loop

The calm leader’s signal loop looks something like this:

StepThe question
NoticeWhat changed?
PlaceWhere does this sit in the system?
CompareWhat else should be moving if this matters?
VerifyWhat receipts exist beyond the headline?
DecideIgnore, watch or do?
ArchitectWhat system needs to change if the signal is real?
CommunicateWhat do people need to know without inheriting unnecessary panic?

That last step matters.

Because the leader’s job is not simply to be calm internally.

It is to prevent unnecessary panic from becoming the organization’s operating system.

The signal loop does not slow the company down.

It keeps motion from becoming waste.

What this looks like in a weekly operating rhythm

Practically, I think this becomes a weekly rhythm.

DayThe question
MondayWhat changed in the market, platforms, customer behavior and capital flows?
TuesdayWhich signals connect to our revenue model?
WednesdayWhat do we ignore, watch or do?
ThursdayWhat system needs updating: content, media, CRM, measurement, product data, sales follow-up, governance?
FridayWhat did we learn, and what would make us change our mind next week?

That is not over-processing.

That is how you keep the organization from turning every headline into a fire drill.

It is also how you build memory.

A team that never records what it saw, what it believed and what it decided has to relearn the same chaos every week.

A team with a signal rhythm compounds.

What the next era rewards

Who wins?

I don’t think the companies with the most AI tools necessarily win.

I don’t think the marketing teams producing the most content win.

I don’t think the leaders reacting fastest win.

I think the winners will be organizations that develop three capabilities simultaneously:

01

Signal intelligence

The ability to identify meaningful changes before they become obvious.

02

Operating architecture

The ability to change systems when those signals matter.

03

Leadership steadiness

The ability to do both without sending the organization into permanent crisis mode.

That third one matters more than it sounds.

Because organizations learn the emotional operating system of their leader.

If everything scares the leader, everything becomes urgent. If everything becomes urgent, prioritization disappears. When prioritization disappears, resources fragment. When resources fragment, nothing compounds.

And then everyone works harder to compensate for a system that no longer knows what matters.

I have another old line for that:

If everything feels urgent, nothing actually is.

That may be one of the most valuable operating principles a CMO can carry into the next few years.

Calm creates compounding

Calm creates compounding.

Not because calm is slow.

Because calm protects focus long enough for the right work to repeat.

The right signal gets tracked. The right system gets improved. The right content gets reused. The right data gets cleaned. The right team behavior gets reinforced.

Panic creates motion. Calm creates accumulation.

That is the part that gets misunderstood.

Calm is not a personality trait. Calm is an operating advantage.

It protects the compounding work from being interrupted by every new anxiety.

And compounding work is where durable advantage usually lives.

What this means for marketing leaders

Marketing may be one of the clearest places to see the difference between signal and noise.

Every week brings another declaration that search is dead, AI agents have replaced the funnel, a platform has changed everything, or an entirely new operating model is required immediately.

Some of those developments will matter enormously.

Most will not matter on the timeline the headline suggests.

The leadership challenge is learning to tell the difference.

Marketing is moving from a traffic-acquisition model toward a visibility, influence and conversion-systems model. People now discover businesses through Google, AI answers, marketplaces, Reddit, TikTok, YouTube, Maps, reviews and recommendation systems. A customer may encounter a company several times without generating a visit that conventional analytics can attribute.

That makes old measurements incomplete. It does not make them useless.

  • A decline in traffic is not automatically a decline in influence.
  • A ranking is not necessarily demand.
  • A viral post is not necessarily commercial momentum.
  • An AI citation is not necessarily revenue.
  • A beautiful attribution dashboard is not proof that the business understands how customers are actually choosing.

This is where calm becomes an analytical advantage.

The calm marketing leader can hold several conflicting signals at once. They can acknowledge that discovery is changing without rebuilding the entire strategy every Tuesday. They can invest in what is already structurally important while watching weaker signals mature.

They can say:

  • Here is what changed.
  • Here is what the evidence supports.
  • Here is what remains uncertain.
  • Here is what we are doing now.
  • Here is what we are watching.
  • Here is the threshold that would cause us to move.

That is how you operationalize ambiguity.

The marketers who win will understand the complete system underneath growth:

Market signal. Customer intent. Brand discovery. Offer. Conversion. Fulfillment. Retention. Margin.

AI can accelerate pieces of that system. It cannot make an incoherent system coherent. It will amplify whatever already exists.

  • Strong data becomes more useful.
  • Weak data becomes more dangerous.
  • Clear positioning travels further.
  • Generic content disappears into the supply.
  • Good operational systems create leverage.
  • Broken systems automate confusion.

The real advantage will belong to leaders who can see how the pieces connect before the market reaches consensus.

What marketing leadership means in 2026 and beyond

I think the CMO role is quietly moving away from chief campaign officer.

And maybe even away from the traditional definition of chief marketing officer.

The future leader looks more like:

  • market intelligence
  • customer intelligence
  • systems architecture
  • capital allocation
  • brand stewardship
  • growth orchestration

They need to understand what people want. They also need to understand how machines find information about what people want.

They need to understand media. They also need to understand data architecture.

They need to understand creative. They also need to understand automation.

They need to understand search. They also need to understand how AI changes discovery.

They need to understand revenue. They also need to understand the upstream economic conditions that create or destroy demand.

And they have to translate all of that for people who want a very simple answer:

What should we do?

That is why the most valuable marketing leader may not be the person with the most answers.

It may be the person who can say:

  • Here is what we know.
  • Here is what we don’t know.
  • Here is what changed.
  • Here is what did not.
  • Here is what I am watching.
  • Here is what would make me change my mind.
  • And here is what we’re doing until then.

That is calm under pressure.

Not pretending uncertainty does not exist.

Giving uncertainty structure.

Data over drama

One of the framework names sitting inside my larger leadership work is:

Data Over Drama.

It fits here too.

Not data instead of humanity. Not dashboards instead of judgment.

Data instead of unnecessary panic. Signal instead of noise. Systems instead of heroics. Curiosity instead of certainty theater. Long-term thinking instead of constant reaction.

Data over drama does not mean dashboards over humanity.

It means evidence over unnecessary panic.

That is the kind of leadership I think the next era requires.

Because there will always be another headline. Another technology. Another disruption. Another quarter. Another thing that supposedly changes everything.

We cannot build organizations that require the leader’s nervous system to catch fire every time the environment changes.

The leader cannot be the emergency response system.

The leader has to build the system.

And maybe that is the thread running underneath far more of my work than I understood at the time.

Search is no longer a page.
Marketing is becoming infrastructure.
Infrastructure cues are upstream indicators.
Human strategy becomes orchestration.
You cannot outwork a broken system.

And the strongest leaders are not the ones who make chaos disappear.

They are the ones who can stand inside it long enough to see what is actually happening.

I wrote it years ago:

I see more. I pause more.

In 2026 and beyond, that may be exactly who wins.
