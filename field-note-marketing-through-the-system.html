FRDTLAB · Field Note · How it works

Marketing through the system, not on top of it.

By Amanda Friedt

If you run social, SEO, and paid for a business by yourself, you already know how to build a campaign structure. An AI operating layer is the same discipline pointed at your own work. Here is the whole thing in one picture and six stages, in words you already use.

The one idea. The model is not the system. It's the router. Everything that makes the output sound like you, use your data, and land where you actually publish lives in the layers around it. Most people set up the router and skip the layers, then wonder why every session starts from zero.

Why the drive is the bus

One copy. Many readers.

Every tool you use has its own memory: the chat app remembers your account, the desktop app remembers this machine, a session remembers itself until it closes. None of those can see each other. A shared drive is the one place all of them, plus your automation tool, plus a scheduled run at 7am, plus a human on your team, can read and write the same file.

You already trust this pattern. Your product feed is the single source of truth that Merchant Center, the ad account, and the site all read from. Nobody maintains three catalogs. The operating layer just applies that rule to how the business works: brand rules, decisions, briefs, deliverables. One copy, many readers.

If you're a one-person shop

Set up these three things first.

01 · Brief

A registry file

One document that lists every brand or client you touch: a short code, where its files live, what the current gate is, who owns it. Any tool that reads it starts informed.

02 · Route

A voice kernel per brand

Not a 40-page brand book. A one-page rule set: register, banned words, claims you can't make, the CTA pattern. It's what keeps a local clinic's copy from sounding like a parts retailer's.

03 · Naming

A naming rule

Files, tasks, and chats carry the brand code and the date in the name. Naming is routing. A file called "final-v3.docx" belongs to nobody; a file called ACME_Report_2026-09.docx files itself.

Do those three and the rest (data pipes, scheduled runs, task-board mirrors) has somewhere to attach. Skip them and you're building automation on sand.

What changes about your job

You hold judgment. The system holds state.

  • You stop being the memory. The registry and the drive hold state. You hold judgment.
  • You stop being the QA step. Guardrails are encoded in the route stage; the system checks health claims, banned words, and source citations before you see the draft.
  • You review at the gate, not the keyboard. Agents draft and stage; nothing client-facing ships until a human flips it. That single rule is what lets the rest run unattended.
  • Your reporting gets honest by default. Every number traces to a pipe. Gaps get flagged as "awaiting data" instead of being smoothed over.

Three things people get wrong.

  • "Better prompts." A prompt is one request. The layer is what makes the thousandth request as good as the first without re-explaining. Prompts are stage 4; most of the payoff is in stages 1, 2, and 6.
  • "One tool to rule them all." You'll use several. The question isn't which one; it's whether they all read the same drive. If they do, switching costs nothing.
  • "Automate first." Automation runs on top of structure. A scheduled run can only act on brands whose brief, voice, and home are filled in. Empty slot, skipped work.

Bottom line. You are not learning a new discipline. You are applying account structure, negatives, feeds, and change history to your own operation. The model does the typing. The layers do the remembering. That's marketing through the system.
